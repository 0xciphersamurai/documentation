# The Limitation

Prediction markets are powerful. They aggregate information, create incentives for truth-telling, and produce remarkably accurate forecasts. Platforms like Polymarket have proven that markets can predict elections, economics, and events better than experts.

**But they're fundamentally limited.**

## The Oracle Problem

Every prediction market needs an oracle - a trusted source to determine the final outcome. Without it, how do you know who wins and loses?

**For objective events, oracles work great:**

| Event Type | Oracle | Example |
|------------|---------|---------|
| Elections | Official results | "Who wins the 2024 presidential election?" |
| Sports | League officials | "Which team wins the Super Bowl?" |
| Market prices | Price feeds | "Will BTC hit $100K by December 31?" |
| Company metrics | Quarterly reports | "Will Apple's revenue exceed $100B this quarter?" |

When there's a clear, objective outcome that everyone agrees on, oracles solve the problem cleanly.

**But most important questions aren't like this.**

## The Questions That Matter More

Think about the decisions you actually see organizations struggling with:

**Strategic Decisions**
- Should the protocol focus on enterprise or consumer adoption?
- Is now the right time to expand internationally?
- Should we build feature X or feature Y first?

**Resource Allocation**
- Which grants deserve DAO funding?
- Where should the research team focus?
- What initiatives get budget priority?

**Technical Choices**
- Which tech stack is best for this project?
- Should we optimize for speed or flexibility?
- What architecture will scale best?

**Cultural Questions**
- What values should define the community?
- Which proposals align with the mission?
- What direction should the movement take?

Notice something? **None of these have objective answers.** There's no oracle that can tell you the "right" choice. Different stakeholders have legitimate disagreements. Multiple perspectives can all be valid.

These are subjective questions. And they're everywhere.

## Why This Matters

Organizations spend enormous resources on subjective decisions:

**Companies** hold endless meetings debating strategic direction. Committees form to evaluate options. Consultants are hired to provide opinions. Executive teams argue in boardrooms.

**DAOs** face the same challenges but worse. Governance forums fill with debate. Snapshot votes happen with minimal participation. Proposals pass or fail based on whale votes rather than collective intelligence.

**Communities** argue in Discord and Twitter. Rough consensus emerges slowly through informal social processes. Leaders make calls but without real confidence in community support.

**Research Labs** struggle to prioritize. Which directions are most promising? What deserves funding? Decisions get made by committees or individuals, often badly.

All this inefficiency exists because **we lack good tools for aggregating opinions on subjective questions.**

## Why Traditional Approaches Fail

Let's examine each existing method:

### Voting

Simple majority votes on subjective questions fail badly:

- **Binary choices** force false dichotomies ("Pick A or B" when the truth is "some of both")
- **No skin in the game** means people vote carelessly
- **Vulnerable to manipulation** through turnout campaigns and coordination
- **Ignores intensity** - someone with deep knowledge votes the same as someone guessing
- **Political gamesmanship** dominates actual evaluation

### Committee Decisions

Small groups making calls for larger organizations:

- **Information bottlenecks** - committee doesn't have distributed knowledge
- **Capture risk** - small groups are easier to influence
- **Slow and bureaucratic** - meetings, deliberation, consensus among committee
- **Accountability problems** - who's responsible when it goes wrong?
- **Limited diversity** - committee members think similarly

### Surveys and Polls

Ask people what they think:

- **No incentive for honesty** - why invest effort in thoughtful answers?
- **Can't capture confidence** - "I'm 60% sure" vs "I'd bet my house on it" look the same
- **Cheap talk** - easy to express opinions, no consequences for being wrong
- **Gaming incentives** - brigading, multiple responses, strategic answers
- **Static snapshots** - can't adapt to new information

### Open Debate

Let people argue it out:

- **Volume doesn't equal insight** - loudest voices dominate
- **Exhausting participants** - most people don't have time for endless discussions
- **No clear resolution** - debates rarely produce definitive conclusions
- **Hard to measure consensus** - is agreement forming or are people just tired?
- **Information gets lost** - key insights buried in long threads

## The Core Problem

All these methods share a fatal flaw: **they don't create proper incentives for revealing information truthfully.**

In a prediction market on an objective event, you're incentivized to trade based on your true beliefs because you profit when you're right. That profit motive creates honest information aggregation.

But on subjective questions? There's no "right answer" to profit from. Traditional methods give you no reason to think carefully, reveal your true beliefs, or stake your reputation on your judgment.

**This is the limitation.** It's not a small problem - it's the reason organizations struggle with most of their important decisions.

## What We Need

To solve subjective decision-making at scale, we need a mechanism that:

1. **Aggregates distributed information** - Like prediction markets do
2. **Creates skin-in-the-game incentives** - Like betting does
3. **Works without objective outcomes** - Unlike traditional markets
4. **Scales to large groups** - Unlike committees
5. **Produces clear signals** - Unlike open debates
6. **Adapts continuously** - Unlike one-time votes

Is this even possible? Can you have a market without an oracle? Can subjective questions have market prices?
