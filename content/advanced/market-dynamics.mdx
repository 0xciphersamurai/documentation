# Market Dynamics

Trigger Market uses the L² norm (Euclidean distance) as its cost function. This choice creates unique market dynamics that encourage consensus formation. Let's understand why.

## The Cost Function

**Formula:** C = √(Σq²_i)

Where:
- C = Total cost (vault value)
- q_i = Quantity of opinion i
- Σ = Sum across all active opinions

**In words:** The cost is the square root of the sum of squared quantities.

**Why this specific formula?** Three key properties:

## Property 1: Diminishing Marginal Returns

### What It Means

The more of an opinion exists, the cheaper it becomes to add more (in relative terms).

**Example:**

**Small opinion (q = 10):**
- Cost to add 1 unit: Higher marginal cost
- Price impact: Significant
- Liquidity: Lower

**Large opinion (q = 50):**
- Cost to add 1 unit: Lower marginal cost
- Price impact: Smaller
- Liquidity: Higher

### Why This Helps Consensus

**Discourages extremes:**
- Very high prices (one dominant opinion): Expensive to push higher
- Very low prices (tiny opinions): Expensive to push lower
- Sweet spot: Balanced distribution

**Encourages diversity:**
- Easy to support underdogs (cheap to add)
- Hard to create monopolies (expensive at high q)
- Natural balance emerges

**Promotes stability:**
- Large opinions resistant to manipulation
- Small opinions easy to boost
- System self-corrects toward balance

## Property 2: Smooth Price Curves

### What It Means

Prices change continuously and predictably as quantities change. No sudden jumps.

**Marginal price formula:**
p_i = q_i / C = q_i / √(Σq²_j)

**Properties:**
- Differentiable everywhere
- No discontinuities
- No edge cases with infinite prices
- Predictable slippage

### Why This Matters

**Predictable trading:**
- You can calculate exact slippage before trading
- No surprises
- Better decision-making

**Stable market making:**
- Smooth curves = easier to provide liquidity
- No sudden shocks
- Reduces manipulation vectors

**Fair pricing:**
- Price reflects true marginal cost
- No arbitrary jumps
- Economically rational

## Property 3: Convergence Pressure

### What It Means

The L² norm creates mathematical pressure toward stable equilibria.

**How it works:**

**When opinions have widely different quantities:**
- High variance in distribution
- Large price disparities
- High trading incentives
- System far from equilibrium

**When quantities converge:**
- Lower variance
- Prices stabilizing
- Fewer trading incentives
- Approaching equilibrium

### The Mechanism

**If Opinion A is overpriced relative to opinion B:**
1. Traders notice arbitrage opportunity
2. Sell A (decreases q_A, increases p_B relatively)
3. Buy B (increases q_B, decreases p_A relatively)
4. Process continues until prices reflect consensus
5. Equilibrium reached when no profitable trades remain

**The L² norm ensures:**
- Arbitrage opportunities diminish as equilibrium approaches
- Final state is stable (no incentive to trade further)
- Convergence is guaranteed given rational traders

### Practical Example

**Start state:**
- Opinion A: q = 60, p ≈ 60%
- Opinion B: q = 30, p ≈ 30%
- Opinion C: q = 10, p ≈ 10%

**If community actually believes A and B are equally good:**
- Traders buy B (cheaper, undervalued)
- Traders sell A (expensive, overvalued)
- Process continues...

**End state (equilibrium):**
- Opinion A: q = 45, p ≈ 45%
- Opinion B: q = 45, p ≈ 45%
- Opinion C: q = 10, p ≈ 10%

**Key insight:** The math makes it profitable to push toward true consensus.

## Liquidity Dynamics

### Depth vs. Constant Product

**Traditional AMM (like Uniswap):**
- Constant product: x × y = k
- Liquidity concentrated in middle
- Extreme prices have terrible liquidity
- Works for 2-asset pools

**L² norm (Trigger Market):**
- Works natively for N opinions (not just 2)
- Liquidity proportional to √(total quantity)
- All opinions benefit from shared liquidity
- No "isolated" positions

### Shared Liquidity Pool

**Key difference:** Every opinion shares the same liquidity pool.

**Impact:**
- Adding liquidity to Opinion A improves liquidity for all opinions
- Trading Opinion A affects Opinion B's price (correlation)
- System-wide liquidity, not per-pair
- More efficient capital usage

**Example:**
- 10 opinions in debate
- Total liquidity shared across all 10
- Adding $100 to Opinion A improves liquidity for all
- Compare to: 45 separate pairs in traditional AMM (10 choose 2)

## Price Sensitivity

### Relationship to Quantity

Price sensitivity (slippage per unit) depends on current quantities:

**Low quantity (q = 5):**
- High sensitivity
- Large price impact per unit
- Illiquid
- Suitable for small positions

**Medium quantity (q = 25):**
- Moderate sensitivity
- Reasonable price impact
- Decent liquidity
- Suitable for most trading

**High quantity (q = 80):**
- Low sensitivity
- Small price impact per unit
- High liquidity
- Suitable for large positions

### Strategic Implications

**For traders:**
- Large positions: Buy gradually (reduce impact)
- Small opinions: Expect higher slippage
- Timing matters: After others trade, liquidity changes

**For stakeholders:**
- Want more liquidity? Increase quantity (buy or stake)
- Defending position? Add liquidity naturally defends
- Shared liquidity helps everyone

## Convergence Timeline

### Early Phase (Days 0-7)

**Characteristics:**
- High volatility
- Large price swings
- Active discovery
- High trading volume

**Why:**
- Information being incorporated
- Participants learning
- Positions being established
- Far from equilibrium

**Opportunity:** Most profit potential, but highest risk

### Middle Phase (Days 7-30)

**Characteristics:**
- Moderate volatility
- Smaller price movements
- Refinement happening
- Steady volume

**Why:**
- Main positions established
- New information causes adjustments
- Approaching equilibrium
- Some stability emerging

**Opportunity:** Balanced risk/reward, still tradeable

### Late Phase (Days 30+)

**Characteristics:**
- Low volatility
- Minimal price changes
- Near settlement criteria
- Lower volume

**Why:**
- Consensus largely formed
- Most information priced in
- At or near equilibrium
- Settlement approaching

**Opportunity:** Low risk, low reward, good for holds

## Manipulation Resistance

### Why L² Norm Helps

**1. Cost scaling:**
- Manipulating prices requires moving quantities
- Cost scales with √(magnitude)
- Larger manipulations disproportionately expensive

**2. Reversion pressure:**
- Artificial prices create arbitrage opportunities
- Other traders profit by correcting
- Manipulator loses to arbitrageurs
- Self-correcting system

**3. Shared liquidity:**
- Can't isolate one opinion pair
- Manipulation affects whole market
- More eyes watching
- Harder to hide

### Attack Examples

**Pump attack (inflate one opinion):**
- Attacker buys large amount of Opinion A
- Price rises, q_A increases
- Cost: Scales with √(q_A)
- Result: Others sell A back down (arbitrage)
- Outcome: Attacker loses on round-trip

**Dump attack (crash one opinion):**
- Attacker sells large amount of Opinion B
- Price falls, q_B decreases
- Cost: Opportunity cost of sold position
- Result: Others buy B back up (cheap)
- Outcome: Attacker sold low, others profit

**Wash trading (fake volume):**
- Attacker trades back and forth
- Cost: Transaction fees on every trade
- Result: No lasting price impact (equilibrium stays)
- Outcome: Attacker pays fees for nothing

**The math protects honest participants.**

## Comparison to Alternatives

### Why Not Linear Cost?

C = Σq_i (simple sum)

**Problems:**
- Prices don't depend on distribution
- No price discovery mechanism
- No convergence pressure
- Doesn't work for consensus markets

### Why Not Constant Product?

C = Π(q_i) (product, like Uniswap)

**Problems:**
- Only works for 2 opinions (extending to N is messy)
- Creates isolated pairs (N choose 2 = many pairs)
- Liquidity fragmentation
- Complex arbitrage across pairs

### Why L² Norm?

**Advantages:**
- Works elegantly for any number of opinions
- Shared liquidity pool
- Smooth, predictable prices
- Convergence properties
- Manipulation resistant
- Value conservation (Euler's theorem)
- Closed-form solutions (gas efficient)

**The choice isn't arbitrary—it's mathematically optimal for multi-opinion consensus markets.**

## Equilibrium States

### Stable Equilibrium

**Characteristics:**
- All opinions priced fairly relative to collective belief
- No arbitrage opportunities
- Low volatility
- Matches settlement criteria

**How reached:**
- Trading drives toward this state
- Arbitrageurs eliminate mispricings
- Convergence theorem guarantees approach
- Time allows discovery

### Unstable Equilibrium

**Characteristics:**
- Prices don't reflect true collective belief
- Arbitrage opportunities exist
- High volatility
- Not sustainable

**Why temporary:**
- Profitable to trade away from
- Natural reversion to stable state
- Can't persist long-term

### Multiple Equilibria?

**Question:** Could there be multiple valid equilibria?

**Answer:** For given beliefs, equilibrium is unique.

**But:** Beliefs can change (new information), creating new equilibrium.

**The system tracks collective belief toward whatever the current true equilibrium is.**

## Practical Trading Insights

### From Market Dynamics

**1. Trade with the convergence:**
- Identify mispriced opinions
- Buy undervalued, sell overvalued
- Let convergence do the work
- Profit from correction

**2. Watch quantity distributions:**
- Extremely uneven = more volatility coming
- Relatively even = approaching stability
- Use as settlement signal

**3. Liquidity scales with participation:**
- More active debates = better liquidity
- Better liquidity = tighter spreads
- Network effects in action

**4. Time is on truth's side:**
- Short-term: Noise and volatility
- Long-term: Convergence to consensus
- Patience rewarded

**5. Share liquidity means everything affects everything:**
- Understand full market, not just your opinion
- Rotations between opinions are common
- System-level thinking helps
